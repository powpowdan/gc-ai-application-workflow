# STAR GUIDE: GCjobs Screening & Interview Answers

How to write answers that pass Government of Canada screening. Read once
fully. Before each application, reread sections 3 and 6.

---

## 1. How GC screening actually scores you

```
   Posting closes
        │
        ▼
   HR screener (+ board member) opens your application
        │
        ▼
   For EACH screening question, they read ONLY that answer     ← not your resume
        │                                                        not your profile
        ▼
   Score: MEETS / DOES NOT MEET  (no partial credit, no averaging)
        │
        ▼
   One "does not meet" on an ESSENTIAL qualification = application rejected
```

Key consequences:

- **The answer is the only evidence.** If an example is on your resume but not in
  the answer, it does not exist. Never write "as stated in my resume."
- **It is pass/fail, not a contest.** You are not trying to be the best answer
  they read. You are trying to make it effortless for a screener to check the
  box. Clarity beats brilliance.
- **Screeners are often not technical.** HR staff screen first against the
  wording of the qualification. Use the posting's own vocabulary in your first
  sentence, then prove it.
- **Asset qualifications get screened too** (to rank or cut large pools). Answer
  every question, including "optional" asset ones: they are free ranking points.

## 2. STAR anatomy

| Part | Job | Share of answer |
|------|-----|-----------------|
| **S (Situation)** | 1-2 sentences of context. Enough to understand the stakes, no more. | ~15% |
| **T (Task)** | What YOU were responsible for. The assignment, not the team's. | ~10% |
| **A (Action)** | What YOU did, step by step. The heart of the answer. | ~50% |
| **R (Result)** | The outcome, with a number or observable fact when possible. Plus closure: what you verified, what changed after. | ~25% |

### Worked example (synthetic; replace with your own record)

**Typical question:** *"Describe your experience managing competing priorities
to deliver a project under tight deadlines."*

**Weak (fails):**

> At my department I worked on the public website for several years. Our team
> was responsible for publishing documents quickly and accurately. I helped
> with updates and worked with the communications team when changes came in.
> It was a high-pressure environment and we always delivered on time.

Why it fails: no specific instance ("several years" is a summary, not an
example), "we" instead of "I", no concrete actions, "helped" is not an action
verb, no verifiable result.

**Strong (passes):**

> (S) During the 2024 release of a high-traffic policy publication at my
> department, last-minute content changes required urgent website updates
> hours before publication under a strict embargo. (T) As Lead Developer, I
> was responsible for implementing and verifying rapid-response code changes
> without breaking live content. (A) I implemented the HTML/CSS/JS updates,
> ran structural and accessibility (WCAG 2.2 AA) verification on every changed
> page, coordinated directly with the requesting office to confirm content
> priorities, and used our Git workflow to stage and safely roll back
> changes. (R) All updates deployed error-free and on schedule; across four
> release cycles as Lead, the site sustained peak traffic with zero downtime.

~780 characters. Notice: first sentence already proves deadline + pressure;
every action verb belongs to "I"; the result closes the loop.

## 3. The rules that fail people

1. **"I", not "we".** They score your role. If the sentence is true when your
   name is removed, it is a team sentence. Convert: "I led", "I built",
   "I verified", "I coordinated".
2. **One example per answer.** A career summary proves nothing. Pick the single
   strongest instance. If you have two, one goes in a different question's
   answer.
3. **Lead with the point.** First sentence should mirror the qualification's
   wording: the question asks about troubleshooting under pressure, sentence
   one contains a problem, pressure, and your role.
4. **Quantify anything a screener can feel.** Time saved, pages published,
   traffic sustained, number of cycles, users of a tool. If you do not know the
   number, use an honest observable ("eliminated manual HTML page creation
   entirely").
5. **Recency and significance.** Prefer the last 5 years. "Significant
   experience" in a posting usually means depth and duration: check the
   poster's own definition; some processes count personal projects, some do
   not (see section 5).
6. **Never paste resume prose.** The resume lists; the answer narrates. Same
   facts, different mode.
7. **Answer the question asked.** If it says "developing AND maintaining", your
   example must show both. Screeners check off each component of the wording:
   split the qualification into its parts before drafting and make sure each
   part appears. If one verb in a combined qualification has no honest
   backing at any ceiling level, substitute the honest verb set in the
   mirror sentence ("configuring, deploying and maintaining" instead of
   "installing, configuring and maintaining") rather than claiming all
   verbs.
8. **Every fact must survive an interview probe.** If asked "walk me through
   how you did that", you must be able to speak to every claim for five
   minutes. Draft only what you can defend.
9. **Yes/no questions get yes/opening-sentence treatment.** GC screening
   questions are often phrased "Do you have experience...". Open with:
   *"Yes, I have experience [mirrors the question's wording verbatim] in my
   position as [title] ([classification]) for [department] ([dates])."* Then
   one STAR example. The mirrored first sentence lets a non-technical
   screener check the box before reading further. When the experience spans
   multiple positions, use the numbered-positions header instead:
   *"Yes, I have experience in X. I acquired this experience in the following
   positions: 1. [Title] ([classification]), [Department], [Month Year] to
   present. 2. [Title] ([classification]), [Department], [Month Year] to
   [Month Year]."* Then the example. (Both conventions appear in
   `old-screening-questions.md`.)

## 4. Recognizing qualification types

| Type | Looks like | Answer strategy |
|------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Experience** | "Experience developing...", "Experience providing..." | One strong STAR example. The workhorse of screening. |
| **Knowledge** | "Knowledge of web accessibility standards" | State the knowledge explicitly, then a brief example of applying it. Interviews test this orally; screening questions may ask you to describe it. |
| **Ability** | "Ability to analyze and solve problems", "ability to communicate orally" | Mostly assessed at interview. Screening answers still need examples if asked. |
| **Personal suitability** | "Initiative", "judgement", "teamwork", "effective interpersonal skills" | Concrete instance of the behaviour, not self-description. "I noticed X, so I did Y" beats "I am a proactive person." |
| **Asset** | "Experience with X would be an asset" | Same STAR treatment. Answer even if optional: ranking fuel. |

Same evidence adapts across types: one strong delivery story can work as
experience (deadline delivery), knowledge (the relevant standards), ability
(problem-solving under pressure), and personal suitability (judgement).

## 5. When personal projects count

Read the poster's experience-source language before using personal examples:

- **"Experience may be gained through..."/"including through personal
  projects, education..."**: personal examples are fair game for experience
  questions.
- **Silent poster**: personal projects are risky for *paid work experience*
  questions but acceptable for skill/knowledge/asset questions. Lead with work
  examples wherever possible.
- **Framing rule:** label honestly ("in independent development work, I...")
  and keep the same STAR rigor. A shipped app with real users, versioned
  releases, and regression tests is a legitimate engineering example.

Your work-backed stories should cover most application streams without
leaving the office. Use personal projects for the skills your day job does
not show: for example mobile development, testing frameworks, cloud
deployment ownership, or Linux operations.

## 6. Character-limit craft

Typical fields allow ~1000 characters. Discipline:

1. Draft the full answer, then cut to fit. Never let the field truncate you:
   the screener sees the cut, not what you wrote.
2. Cut in this order: adjectives, background, second examples, "successfully/
   effectively" adverbs. Never cut: actions, results, the "I" verbs.
3. Abbreviate only after first full use: "Minister's Office (MINO)".
4. Numbers are characters well spent: "4 release cycles", "zero downtime",
   "eliminated manual page creation".
5. Final read: would a non-technical screener, skimming, check the box in
   30 seconds? Strong first-sentence structure does this in plain text.

---

## 7. Interview mode

- The bank you build for screening doubles as interview prep. Interviews probe
  the same stories: expect "walk me through it", "what would you do
  differently", "what was the hardest part".
- Know two layers deep: the STAR answer is layer one; the how (tools used,
  why this approach, alternatives rejected) is layer two.
- Personal suitability questions want recent, specific behaviours: same
  rule as screening, spoken.
- Bring your own numbers: magnitudes you verified during drafting are exactly
  what interview answers need.

---

## Pre-submission checklist (per answer)

```
[ ] First sentence mirrors the qualification wording
[ ] One specific example, within the last 5 years if possible
[ ] Actions are "I" verbs, not "we"
[ ] Every component of the qualification's wording is addressed
[ ] Result stated, with a number or observable fact
[ ] Every claim defensible for 5 minutes under probe
[ ] Fits the character limit with room to spare
[ ] Personal-project evidence only where the poster allows / role permits
```
